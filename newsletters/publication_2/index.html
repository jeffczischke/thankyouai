The tell

A Fortune 500 retailer's CIO walked me through their AI roadmap recently. Three years of work. Copilot deployed across the enterprise. A custom LLM interface their internal team had built. Pilots in finance, operations, supply chain. The kind of roadmap most companies are still trying to get approved.

What he said next

"The excitement is gone. People are going back to their old spreadsheets."

That sentence is the tell. Not the hallucinations, though those came up too. Not the auditor questions, though those were real. The tell is when employees who were genuinely energized by early results quietly decide the old way is more reliable. That shift is hard to reverse once it sets in. And it happens for one reason more than any other.

The architecture never had a foundation.

The model is doing exactly what you told it to do.

When AI outputs are inconsistent, the instinct is to blame the model. Upgrade the prompt. Try a different LLM. Buy a better tool. I understand the instinct. It's where the money goes and it's where the vendor conversations happen.

But most of the time, the model is performing correctly. It's probabilistic. It generates the most statistically likely answer based on what it can see. If what it can see is incomplete, inconsistently governed, or stitched together from whatever data happened to be accessible at deployment time, inconsistent outputs aren't a bug. They're the expected result.

Most enterprise GenAI deployments skip the deterministic layer: a structured, governed, auditable data foundation that sits between your raw operational data and your LLM. Jeff Czischke · The Foundation Problem

That's where facts live. Where business rules get enforced programmatically instead of hoped for probabilistically. Where exceptions route to a human instead of quietly producing a confident-sounding wrong answer in a finance report.

Without it, you're asking a probabilistic system to do a deterministic job. It will fail on a schedule. And your best people will notice first.

The conversation I have with every CIO

What actually changes, and what stays exactly as it is.

You don't have to throw any of it away. The interface your teams use every day stays. Your Microsoft EA, your Anthropic agreement, your OpenAI contract: all intact. No new vendor relationships. No procurement reviews. No change management project to convince people to use a different interface.

What changes is what sits underneath.

The objection

"Our data isn't clean enough."

I hear this in almost every first conversation. I understand why it sounds responsible. In practice, I've watched it delay meaningful work for a year or more while organizations wait for a data governance initiative that never quite reaches done.

The automation programs I've seen deliver finance-grade results were built on existing ERP data. No pre-cleanup program. No migration project before anyone was allowed to start. The data was already good enough. It just needed the right architecture to make it usable.

That distinction matters. It moves the conversation from "we can't begin until everything is perfect" to "we begin now, on what we have, and we instrument it correctly from Day 1."
Three paths

Three options. Honestly mapped.

When I scope this kind of work, the same three options come up every time. Most organizations don't need the first two.

Path 01

Build from scratch

Net-new infrastructure, designed from the ground up.

  • +Right for greenfield environments or AI investments genuinely broken beyond repair.
  • −Means writing off years of work and training.
  • −Slow. The slowest of the three options.
Best fit Net-new programs with no existing AI investment to protect.
Path 02

Buy a packaged solution

A vendor product that handles one use case end-to-end.

  • +Works for commodity processes with limited customization.
  • −Rarely scales past the first use case.
  • −Builds vendor dependency without building any internal capability.
Best fit A single, well-bounded, commodity process you can wall off from the rest.
Recommended
Path 03

Evolve what you have

Layer deterministic intelligence under the tools you already run.

  • +Protects current contracts and existing investment.
  • +Builds on what your people already know how to use.
  • +Discovery to first production automation in 70 days, same team start to finish.
Best fit Organizations already invested in AI that need those investments to actually perform.
The question worth sitting with

What does our current architecture assume about data reliability, and has that assumption ever actually been tested?

Most organizations made a reasonable assumption when they started their AI journey: that their data was good enough to produce reliable outputs. They just never tested that assumption. When the pilots worked, nobody asked why. When they didn't, everyone blamed the model.

The question isn't "should we build or buy?" It's the one above. If the answer is no, that's where the work starts. And it doesn't require starting over.
