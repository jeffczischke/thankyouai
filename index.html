The GenAI Litmus Test

Three uncomfortable questions every executive must ask their engineering team right now.

The Guardrail Gap

I see it after advising enterprise AI programs across financial services, healthcare, life sciences, and manufacturing: the gap between "we have AI in production" and "we have governable AI in production" is where careers go to die.

The questions below are a three-question diagnostic I use with clients. You don't need a consultant to run them. You need twenty minutes and the intellectual honesty to sit with the answers.

Each question carries a yes/no self-check. Mark your honest answers. Your running score appears at the bottom.

01
Explainability & Audit

If your AI makes an error, can you trace the exact mathematical path it took to get there?

Read that again. Not "can you see the output." Not "can you run it again and see if it happens twice." Can you trace the exact mathematical path the model took to produce a specific, wrong answer?

For a standard LLM, RAG-based or otherwise, the honest answer is no. This isn't a criticism of the technology; it's a structural reality of how transformer models work. Billions of weight multiplications across layers do not preserve a human-readable reasoning chain.

Why it matters beyond the technical: the EU AI Act mandates transparency and traceability for high-risk systems. GDPR Article 22 grants individuals the right to meaningful information about automated decisions. The ACC's 2025 AI Governance Toolkit lists explainability as one of only 13 core principles. "The model is a black box" is not a defensible answer to a board, a regulator, or a court.

Diagnostic: Answer honestly

For every AI-generated output that affects a customer, employee, or business decision, do you have a documented audit trail of what was retrieved, how it was ranked, what the model was instructed to do, and what guardrails were triggered?

02
Architecture vs. duct tape

Are you spending more time writing prompt guardrails than building actual features?

There's a moment in every RAG/LLM project where the team quietly starts losing the battle against the model's own creativity. A customer-facing AI gives an off-brand answer, so you add a guardrail. It hallucinates a spec, so you add another. It gives a technically accurate but legally problematic interpretation, and now there's a Slack thread with Legal.

Three sprints later, engineers spend 60% of their time writing prompt instructions about what the model should not do, and you've shifted from AI product development to AI behavior management.

This isn't a failure of intelligence. It's a signal about architectural fit. Prompt engineering is not a governance strategy. It's duct tape. The teams that have scaled past pilots didn't get there by writing better prompts. They got there by building architecture that enforces constraints at runtime.

"Policy can no longer be a slow governance artifact reviewed in committees. It must become part of the runtime operating model, enforcing constraints at the moment an agent is about to act, not after the fact." Charles Skamser · PX42 enterprise architecture
Diagnostic: Answer honestly

Show the ratio of engineering time spent on guardrail management versus net-new feature capability in the last two sprints. Is the feature line growing faster than the guardrail line?

Step 4 below is the architectural fix: input validation, retrieval-layer ACLs, output evaluators, workflow approval gates.
03
Liability & the 92% problem

What happens to your liability if the AI gives a "less wrong" but still legally actionable answer?

This is the question that makes rooms go quiet. Not "what if the AI is catastrophically wrong." Everyone has thought about that. I'm asking about the more dangerous case: what happens when the AI is 92% right.

Your chatbot answers a coverage question. It correctly names the policy type, the limits, the exclusion period, and misses one jurisdiction-specific carve-out a licensed agent would have flagged. The customer acts on the answer. A claim is denied.

Who owns that? The model doesn't have a law license. The vendor's terms point indemnification back at you. And if you deployed the system without adequate human-in-the-loop checkpoints (checkpoints you replaced with a system prompt telling it to "always refer customers to their policy documents"), you have a paper trail showing you knew the system had limitations and deployed it anyway.

This isn't hypothetical. The ACC AI Toolkit is explicit: lawyers remain personally responsible for AI-generated legal work under their duty of competence. The same accountability logic is being extended across financial services, healthcare, and insurance. Deploy AI into customer-facing workflows without redesigning the accountability model and you don't reduce liability; you diffuse it. Diffused accountability is worse: no one gets the early warning signal when the system drifts. And drift is not if, it's when.

Diagnostic: Answer honestly

Can you produce, today, a written accountability map for every workflow where AI output reaches a customer without mandatory human review, naming who is legally accountable if that output triggers a complaint, inquiry, or litigation?

So what does fixing this look like?

Six concrete steps. Sequenced.

Most thought-leadership pieces stop at the diagnosis. The next six steps are what I've actually seen work when a mature AI Center of Excellence (or a well-led IT organization) takes this seriously.

  1. STEP 01

    Stand up a dedicated AI governance function.Before you scale anything.

    Technology teams optimize to ship. Governance requires someone whose job is to say no, slow things down at the right moments, and maintain a living inventory of every AI system in production.

    IBM's research: organizations with a Chief AI Officer see 10% greater ROI; centralized or hub-and-spoke operating models produce 36% higher ROI than decentralized. You don't need a headcount of 30. You need one empowered person with a cross-functional mandate and a seat at the table.

  2. STEP 02

    Build an AI system inventory before regulators ask for one.The most important early deliverable.

    Sounds like busywork. It's the foundation. Capture, for every AI system: what it does, which data it touches, which decisions it influences, what the human oversight mechanism is, and which regulatory frameworks apply.

    Shadow AI is endemic: more than half of employees say they would use AI without employer authorization. A maintained spreadsheet with a defined review cadence beats a sophisticated platform no one updates.

  3. STEP 03

    Implement runtime observability, not just output logging.The fix for Question 01.

    Capture the full decision trace: what was retrieved, the retrieval scores, what the model was instructed to do, what it actually did. The enterprise standard is a three-tier model:

    Tier 1Business metrics: goal completion, task success, revenue or cost impact. Your board-facing layer.

    Tier 2Application telemetry: latency, error rates, token consumption, human feedback signals. Daily ops layer.

    Tier 3Trace-level debugging: full OpenTelemetry spans for every action, tool call, intermediate step. On-demand. This is how you answer the auditor's question.

  4. STEP 04

    Replace prompt guardrails with architectural controls.The fix for Question 02.

    Prompt-level guardrails are fragile because they are instructions, not enforcement. A creative user input or a vendor model update can bypass them silently. Move controls into the architecture:

    Input layer. A validation agent classifies the request before it reaches the main model: rejects out-of-scope, flags high-risk types for review, logs the decision.

    Retrieval layer. Source-level access controls. Enforced by infrastructure, not a prompt instruction.

    Output layer. An evaluator agent checks generated responses against a defined policy set before they return to the user. Logged, tested, updated independently.

    Workflow layer. Define which actions require human approval before execution. Human-in-the-loop is a feature, not a weakness.

  5. STEP 05

    Define the accountability map in writing before each deployment.The fix for Question 03.

    Before any AI system touching customers or regulated workflows ships, require a completed accountability document covering five fields:

    1. Decision boundary: what is this system authorized to decide, and what is explicitly out of scope?
    2. Error impact classification: worst-case downstream impact if it's wrong; who it affects; how.
    3. Human review requirements: which output categories require human review before reaching the end user.
    4. Accountability assignment: who, by name and role, is accountable for outputs in each error category.
    5. Incident response path: if a compliance issue is triggered, who is notified, in what order, within what timeframe.

  6. STEP 06

    Treat evaluation as a continuous practice, not a pre-launch checklist.Models drift. Quietly.

    The most common governance failure isn't the absence of guardrails at launch. It's the absence of any process to detect when they stop working. A system that was 91% accurate at launch can be 78% accurate six months later, and no one will know unless continuous evaluation was built in from day one.

    Define evaluation metrics before launch. Run automated evaluation on every model revision. Set quality thresholds that trigger a human review process if crossed. Schedule quarterly human evaluation to calibrate whether your automated criteria still reflect what the business values.

The bottom line

The Litmus Test is not meant to stop your AI program. It's meant to surface the gaps that will stop it for you, at the worst possible moment.

When a regulator, a plaintiff's attorney, or your own board comes asking. The organizations scaling AI successfully (and BCG data shows they're outperforming peers by 2.6× on revenue growth and 38% on EBIT) are not moving recklessly and they are not moving cautiously.

They are moving with structured confidence: clear accountability, observable systems, runtime controls, and a governance function empowered to set the pace. That is the standard. The Litmus Test tells you how far you are from it.
